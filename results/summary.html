1. The direct answer

The observed transition lies between loads 0.6 and 1.05. This range applies to this machine, duration and configuration; it is not a universal capacity limit. Load 1 means the processing budget already consumes the entire interarrival interval; reading, conversion and scheduling take additional time.

A growing queue means data arrive faster than they are processed. A larger buffer retains more data, but does not speed up the consumer. A smaller buffer may bound latency by discarding samples: low delay with loss is not success.

2. Watch the backlog build up

A rising queue during transmission means backlog is accumulating. A decline after the red line means pending data are being processed. The processed fraction trails the sent fraction when the consumer falls behind. Each line is an actual run, not an average that hides peaks.

3. Buffer, rate and chunk size: extremes and intermediate values

Buffer: fixed processing load 3. Rate: fixed processing cost of 976.562 µs/sample and a 1 s buffer. Chunk: load 0.25 and buffer of 8 s. These panels vary different parameters and should be interpreted separately. At low rates with a fixed chunk size, packets take longer to form; at high rates with fixed processing cost, the consumer may fall behind. Latency therefore need not vary monotonically with input rate. Queue plots and the delay breakdown distinguish these causes.

What happens when the buffer fills up?

Consumer processing cost is identical across these curves. 100% denotes configured receiver capacity; actual peaks depend on the implementation and queue-count sampling. Missing samples appear as sequence-index gaps. A queue that flattens near capacity, while sequence gaps increase, indicates saturation with discard, not healthy stability. The final point includes total loss when draining completed.

A 1 s buffer stores one second of signal; it does not guarantee a one-second latency limit: a consumer at load 3 needs about three seconds to process that content.

4. Where does total delay come from?

Total = transmitter chunk formation and scheduling + transport/queue until read + batch processing. For each sample, total delay runs from acquisition timestamp to processing completion. p99 is the delay below which 99% of processed samples fall; maximum is the worst observed case. Components below are run-level means of per-run medians; they must not be added to obtain p99. If observation ends with pending data, p99 and maximum cover processed samples only; they do not summarize the unprocessed tail.

Added delay is the difference between the last and first valid production windows, not the sum of all sample delays. Queue ms/s measures how much additional signal time accumulates per wall-clock second. Drain time runs from the end of acquisition to the last processing completion; when incomplete, it is only the observed time, not the time needed to recover.

Nyquist and streams with different rates

Acquisition rate must exceed twice the highest signal frequency, with antialias filtering and a practical margin. This differs from the batch-reading frequency and display grid rate. A fast shared grid does not create new measurements in a slower stream. Offline interpolation cannot undo aliasing or prove real-time availability. Ideal rectangular marker pulses are not band-limited analog signals.

A shared consumer can delay EEG while processing EMG. In an application, separate reading from processing using bounded queues, preserve LSL timestamps and define an explicit sample-age/discard policy. Independent readers cannot fix insufficient consumer capacity; they only move the backlog elsewhere. This experiment keeps the shared consumer to measure competition between streams.

5. Statistical evidence and criteria

Prespecified primary comparison: loads ≥1.5 versus ≤0.6, paired by repetition. Difference in queue growth: 500.7 ms/s (95% bootstrap CI: 499.1 to 503.2); two-sided exact sign test p=0.03125, 6 complete pairs, 6 non-tied. The statistical unit is the run, never an individual sample. Exploratory intervals based on few repetitions are imprecise; other contrasts and the boundary are descriptive, without multiple significance claims.

Medians in windows of 0.5 s, excluding the first window and the entire drain phase. Sustained growth requires at least four windows, queue slope >10 ms/s and total-delay growth >50 ms. For recovery cases, slope and delay growth are calculated during overload only; the return diagnosis uses the sustained criterion shown in the recovery plot. The boundary requires growth in every repetition and a slope CI lower bound above 10 ms/s; the stable bound requires a CI upper bound ≤10 ms/s, with no loss or observation cutoffs.

Queue depth is the available-sample count reported by samples_available(), before reading and after processing. Sampled queue peaks may underestimate peaks between reads. This metric measures neither every internal buffer in bytes nor clock drift. Controlled processing cost is imposed by serial waiting; the experiment measures the consequence of insufficient capacity, not EEG-algorithm performance. Drain observation limit: 8 s, allowing an already-started batch to finish. If data remain at the limit, total loss is unknown. Latency uses the initial LSL clock correction and ends in the Python process, not at a hardware timestamp. Technical failures are never included in averages as zero.
